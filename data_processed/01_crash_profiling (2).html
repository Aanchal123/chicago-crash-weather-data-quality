def value_counts_df(series, colname):
    out = (
        series.fillna("<<MISSING>>")
        .astype(str)
        .str.strip()
        .value_counts(dropna=False)
        .rename_axis(colname)
        .reset_index(name="count")
    )
    out["pct"] = (out["count"] / out["count"].sum() * 100).round(2)
    return out

Semantic Cleaning: Denial Constraints and Cross-Field Consistency¶

The following checks implement denial constraints — rules that identify pairs or groups of field values that should not co-occur if the data are internally consistent (Ilyas & Chu, 2019). Unlike syntactic checks (which verify format and type), semantic checks evaluate whether combinations of values make sense given the real-world process that generated the data.

Three denial constraints are evaluated here, grounded in the IDOT Illinois Traffic Crash Report SR-1050 instruction manual (IDOT, 2019):

  • SR-1050 defines WEATHER_CONDITION as the weather at the time of the crash, as observed by the responding officer.
  • SR-1050 defines ROADWAY_SURFACE_COND as the physical condition of the road surface at the time of the crash.

Because these two fields capture conditions at the same moment but reflect different phenomena (atmospheric vs. physical surface state), certain combinations that appear contradictory may in fact be plausible:

  • Check A (CLEAR + wet/icy surface): Roads can remain wet or icy after precipitation has stopped. CLEAR weather with a wet surface is physically plausible and is not treated as an automatic error.
  • Check B (snow/freezing weather + DRY surface): Less plausible — active snow or freezing rain would typically produce a non-dry surface. Flagged as a higher-suspicion inconsistency.
  • Check C (UNKNOWN/OTHER weather + known surface): Not an error, but an uncertainty indicator. Useful for sensitivity analysis.

].copy()

].copy()

check_C = check_df[
    (check_df["WEATHER_CONDITION"].isin(["UNKNOWN", "OTHER"])) &
    (~check_df["ROADWAY_SURFACE_COND"].isin(["UNKNOWN", "OTHER", "nan"]))
].copy()

Missingness Analysis: MAR, MCAR, and MNAR¶

Missing value analysis goes beyond counting NULLs. The underlying mechanism of missingness determines how missing values should be handled (Ilyas & Chu, 2019):

  • MCAR (Missing Completely At Random): Missingness has no relationship to any observed or unobserved variable. Safe to drop or impute.
  • MAR (Missing At Random): Missingness is related to observed variables but not to the missing value itself. Can be handled with informed imputation using observed predictors.
  • MNAR (Missing Not At Random): Missingness is related to the unobserved value itself or to an unobserved process. Dropping or imputing without care introduces bias.

Crash injury fields (107 missing records): The co-missing check above shows that all 107 records missing INJURIES_TOTAL are also missing MOST_SEVERE_INJURY. These fields are completed together by the reporting officer. Missingness concentrated in records with no severity classification at all suggests these are incomplete crash reports — likely cases where the report was submitted without full injury documentation. This is consistent with MNAR: the missingness is related to the reporting process itself, not to a random mechanism or an observed predictor. These records are retained for crash count totals but excluded from injury severity comparisons.

Location fields (580 missing records): LATITUDE, LONGITUDE, and LOCATION are missing for 580 records (1.08%). These fields are not used in the daily aggregation or downstream wet/dry comparison, so missingness here does not affect the downstream task.

Duplicate Detection: Method and Justification¶

Duplicate detection was applied in two stages:

Stage 1 — Exact duplicate check on CRASH_RECORD_ID: CRASH_RECORD_ID is the system-assigned unique identifier for each crash record in the Chicago Data Portal. Checking for duplicate values on this field is the appropriate primary deduplication method because it reflects the source system's own uniqueness constraint. Zero duplicates were found, confirming identifier integrity.

Stage 2 — Near-duplicate check (same day, location, injuries): A secondary check identified records sharing the same crash_day, rounded latitude/longitude (4 decimal places), and INJURIES_TOTAL value. This catches potential cases where the same crash was submitted twice with different record IDs. 1,266 candidate records were flagged (2.36% of dataset). Inspection showed that 97.2% had INJURIES_TOTAL = 0, 38.6% had missing coordinates (NaN rounds to the same value), and sample review confirmed distinct CRASH_RECORD_IDs and differing field values. These are legitimate distinct crashes at the same intersection — a common occurrence in urban Chicago traffic — not duplicate submissions. All records retained.
