Integration: Heterogeneity Resolution¶

Integrating the crash and weather datasets required resolving heterogeneities at two levels (Ilyas & Chu, 2019):

Schema-level heterogeneity: The two sources use different field names, types, and granularities:

Aspect Crash Dataset Open-Meteo
Date field name CRASH_DATE (datetime string) date (ISO date string)
Date field type String requiring explicit parsing String, direct parse
Granularity One row per crash (53,608 records) One row per day (181 records)
Weather representation Categorical officer-reported labels Numeric daily totals and WMO codes
Geographic scope Individual crash locations Single point (lat 41.88, lon -87.63)

Resolution: crash records were aggregated to daily totals (one row per day) before merging, aligning granularity with Open-Meteo. A shared date key (crash_day) was created from the parsed crash datetime field.

Record-level heterogeneity: The crash dataset uses local Chicago time (America/Chicago). Open-Meteo was requested with timezone=America/Chicago, ensuring both sources use the same daily date boundaries. Crashes near midnight were therefore assigned to the same calendar day in both sources. No timezone correction was required after this alignment.

Join method and quality: A left join on crash_day was used to retain all 181 crash days. All 181 days matched Open-Meteo observations. Zero unmatched crash days. Zero missing weather values after merge.

Impact Assessment: Fitness-for-Use and Scenario Design¶

The central research question asks how quality decisions affect conclusions about crash frequency and injury severity across weather conditions. The downstream task — a daily wet versus dry comparison — is directly affected by two quality issues identified in profiling:

  1. High uncertainty in officer-reported weather fields (8.41% UNKNOWN/OTHER in WEATHER_CONDITION) means a weather classification based solely on crash records will misclassify or exclude a meaningful share of days.
  2. Systematic undercount of wet days under officer-reported classification (Scenario A identifies 37 wet days vs. 94 under Open-Meteo) shows that the choice of weather source materially changes which days are analyzed.

Three scenarios are compared to make this impact explicit:

  • Scenario A uses the officer-reported WEATHER_CONDITION field as the weather source, classifying a day as wet if ≥20% of crash records are coded with a precipitation-type condition.
  • Scenario B uses Open-Meteo precipitation_sum > 0 as the wet day definition — an objective, externally measured threshold.
  • Scenario C applies Scenario B's classification but restricts to days where UNKNOWN/OTHER weather share is ≤10%, providing a robustness check on whether high-uncertainty days are driving the results.

The 20% threshold in Scenario A was chosen as a conservative definition: it requires that at least 1 in 5 crash records on a given day be coded with precipitation weather before the day is classified as wet. Lower thresholds (e.g. 5%) would classify more days as wet but would be more sensitive to individual officer reporting decisions.

# ── SCENARIO A: officer-reported weather classification ──────────────
crash_sel = pd.read_csv(PROCESSED_DIR / "crash_selected_columns.csv")
crash_sel["crash_day"] = pd.to_datetime(crash_sel["crash_day"], errors="coerce")
crash_sel["WEATHER_CONDITION"] = crash_sel["WEATHER_CONDITION"].astype(str).str.strip().str.upper()
crash_sel["INJURIES_TOTAL"] = pd.to_numeric(crash_sel["INJURIES_TOTAL"], errors="coerce")

wet_officer_cats = ["RAIN", "SNOW", "FREEZING RAIN/DRIZZLE", "SLEET/HAIL", "BLOWING SNOW"]
crash_sel["officer_wet"] = crash_sel["WEATHER_CONDITION"].isin(wet_officer_cats).astype(int)

daily_officer = (
    crash_sel.groupby("crash_day")
    .agg(
        daily_crash_count=("CRASH_RECORD_ID", "count"),
        daily_injuries_total=("INJURIES_TOTAL", "sum"),
        officer_wet_count=("officer_wet", "sum"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)
daily_officer["officer_wet_day"] = (
    daily_officer["officer_wet_count"] / daily_officer["daily_crash_count"] >= 0.20
).astype(int)

Fitness-for-Use Conclusion¶

The research question asks how data quality decisions affect conclusions about daily crash frequency and injury severity across weather conditions in Chicago (January–June 2025).

Is the integrated dataset fit for this use?

Yes, with documented limitations:

Crash frequency conclusions are reliable. Daily crash counts are based on 53,608 records with complete crash identifiers and dates. Deduplication confirmed no exact duplicates and near-duplicate candidates were verified as distinct events. The daily aggregation is sound.

Injury severity conclusions require caution. 107 records (0.20%) have missing injury values in a pattern consistent with MNAR — incomplete crash reports missing both injury counts and severity classification. These records are retained in crash count totals but should be noted as a limitation in injury comparisons. More importantly, average daily injuries barely change across wet and dry days under all three scenarios (range: 65.1 to 68.8), suggesting that in this window and at this level of aggregation, injury severity is not meaningfully associated with weather condition.

The weather source choice materially affects exposure classification. Officer-reported weather identifies only 37 wet days (20% of the window) while Open-Meteo identifies 94 (52%). Despite this difference, both sources consistently show higher average daily crashes on wet days. The qualitative conclusion — wet days have more crashes — is stable across all three scenarios. The quantitative estimates differ: Scenario A shows a larger wet/dry gap (322.1 vs 289.5) while Scenario B shows a smaller gap (305.9 vs 285.7), reflecting the different composition of days classified as wet under each approach.

Sensitivity analysis confirms stability. Scenario C, which excludes 44 high-uncertainty days (UNKNOWN/OTHER weather share >10%), produces results consistent with Scenario B (307.3 vs 295.4), confirming that the Open-Meteo-based conclusions are not driven by high-uncertainty days.

Key limitation: The six-month window (January–June 2025) overrepresents winter and spring conditions and excludes summer and fall. Conclusions about wet-day crash patterns may not generalize to the full year.
